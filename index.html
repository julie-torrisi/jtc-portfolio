My direction

No one agreed on what our assets should look like, or where they should live.

FLEX was the most recent attempt at the problem. It was an ambitious system, but when six assets were viewed together, they didn’t yet read as one brand, and leadership held off on approving it for rollout. Without an approved illustration system in place, execution remained blocked across surfaces.

Problems

BusinessOne illustration, four versions. Marketing, product, brand and engineering each owned a slice of how visuals shipped. No one owned the whole thing.
IllustrationFLEX drifted into inconsistency over time, producing assets that no longer read as one brand.
IconographyLoose guardrails meant icons varied across the product, with no shared standard for style or construction.
MotionFile creation and storage practices forced a bad tradeoff: assets were either too heavy, lagging load times, or too low-quality to ship. The styles weren’t always consistent.

How do we systematize illustration, iconography, and animation so they hold up over time?

Talk track · voice-over only
  • Mapped who owned what. I sat with each team and wrote down where their assets came from, who approved them and how they shipped.
  • Found the overlap. Teams were solving the same problem separately. I pulled them into one plan instead of four.
  • Unblocked the designers. Brilliant people, new to product scale. I ran the workshop to give them the constraints up front, so they could ship without guessing.
  • Moved us from a house of brands closer to a branded house. One Facebook, one visual language.

The answers were all over the place. That was the proof we needed.

Modernizing Facebook comments.

As design lead for Comments, I led a seven-month modernization of public conversation for 3 billion people. 9 partner teams. Ship approval from Tom Alison, Head of Facebook.

The opportunity

Facebook was losing the next generation.

By 2025, Facebook’s comment participation rate had declined 12.5% year-over-year.

Instagram, TikTok and YouTube all trending the other way, and their conversations were where young adults went.

Risk

Doing nothing, and losing young adults a little more every year.

What I didTook over comments in late 2024. While on the Creators team we cared a lot about the quality of public conversations.

Context

This wasn’t a product problem. It was a system problem.

Comments read as toxic and the UI was dated. No one owned comments, so every team changed the comment sheet its own way, and it worked differently depending on where you were in the app. Every new feature broke two old ones.
